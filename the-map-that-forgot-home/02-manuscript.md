# The Map That Forgot Home

*Manuscript. Chapters 1–3.*

---

## Chapter One
### Islands That Weren't There

There were four islands outside Wrenmouth harbour that had not been there at lunchtime.

Mara Ellery checked the window. Grey sea. Grey October sky. The harbour wall curled round like an arm holding the fishing boats safe, and the lifeboat station sat at the end of it like an orange full stop. No islands.

She looked back down at the map.

Four islands, plain as anything, just beyond the harbour mouth. They were drawn in a brownish-green ink so fresh that it shone. While Mara watched, a fifth began to appear beside them: first a wobbly outline, as if an invisible nib were feeling its way, then a fringe of tiny houses, then a name in letters so small she had to bend until her nose almost touched the paper.

*Lantern Quay.*

"That," said Mara, "is not allowed."

She said it the way she would have said it about a sum that didn't add up. Things had rules. Maps had more rules than almost anything. A map was supposed to tell you the truth about where things were, and keep telling you, and not add islands when your back was turned.

Twenty minutes ago, the map had simply been the best thing she had ever found.

Mara had spent the first Friday of half-term in the Gull Head lighthouse with a clipboard, a tape measure and a pencil behind each ear. The Wrenmouth Heritage Trust was turning the lighthouse into a museum, and Mrs Bello, whose firm was mending the roof, had said Mara could help as long as she kept out from under the ladders. So Mara had made a plan of the whole building. Every room. Every cupboard. One hundred and eight steps from the front door to the lamp room, counted three times, because the first time she'd been talking and the second time she'd sneezed.

The lamp room was the best part. The great glass lens stood in the middle like an enormous beehive made of ice, and round the walls ran a curved wooden table with wide, flat drawers for charts. All the drawers had been empty except the bottom one, which was stuck.

Mara had not yanked it. Yanking was how things got broken. She had slid her ruler along the gap until it found a little brass catch, and pressed, and waited, and the drawer had sighed open.

Inside were three things.

A spool of silver thread, wound tight as a cotton reel, with a brown luggage label tied to it. On the label, in spiky handwriting: *Mends what's broken. Not what's lost. E.M.*

A fountain pen, its nib crusted with dried ink the colour of seaweed.

And the map.

It had been folded in four, soft as cloth along the creases. When Mara opened it out on the table, her breath caught, because there in the bottom corner was Wrenmouth. *Her* Wrenmouth, drawn in faded black ink by somebody who had loved it. The harbour. Fore Street climbing the hill. St Wenna's with its stubby tower. The lighthouse on Gull Head, with tiny rays drawn round the top. Someone had even drawn a little fish above the building on the harbour front where Ellery's Fish and Chips stood now, and Mara had laughed out loud.

The rest of the paper was empty sea, washed a pale grey-blue. Round the edge ran a border of tiny marks, neat as stitches. Mara had counted them, because she counted things. Seventy-two.

Then she had taken out her sticky notes to copy the fish for Nan.

Nan had come to live with them in April, after the business with the gas ring, and the flat above the shop still muddled her. So Mara drew maps. A yellow square on the bathroom door: a toothbrush and an arrow. One on the fridge: *KETTLE, 2ND CUPBOARD. MUGS ABOVE.* One by Nan's bed with the way to the stairs and a smiley face, because Nan liked the smiley faces best. Nan said they were the best maps in the world. Some days she said it twice, because she had forgotten saying it the first time.

Mara didn't mind.

Mostly.

She had been halfway through the fish when the first island appeared, and now there were five, and the pencil in her hand had gone still.

On the far side of the lens, something went *clunk*.

"Your mum said not to touch that," Mara said, without looking up.

"I'm not touching it." Idris Bello's voice came from somewhere inside the brass frame that held the lens. "I'm investigating it."

"With a spanner."

"With an investigating spanner."

Idris was eleven and had lived in Wrenmouth since July, which was not long enough to learn that you didn't call people *Clipboard*. He had called her Clipboard twice yesterday. Mara had not called him anything back, which she felt showed that she was the better person.

On the map, something new was happening. A thin brownish-green line had begun to creep over Edie's black ones, tracing Fore Street, then the harbour, then the long curve of the sea front. It was checking, Mara thought. Going over the old drawing the way a teacher goes over your homework. And it didn't stop where the black lines stopped. It drew Kestrel Rise, where the new houses were, and the supermarket roundabout, and the skate park. None of those had been there when the black-ink person made this map.

Mara remembered the men in August. Three of them, in long grey coats, measuring the harbour with brass chains that clinked like money. Dad had said they were from the council. Mara had wanted to ask them about the chains, but by the time she'd worked up to it they had gone.

Then the new line did something worse.

It ran out from the lighthouse, out from the very room where she was standing, across the harbour mouth to the new islands. Fine and straight and shining, like the beam of a torch drawn in ink. Letters followed it along, one by one.

*Lamp Road.*

Mara took the pencil from behind her ear and made a tiny dot in the margin. She watched it. It sat there being grey and ordinary and pencil-ish. It didn't shine. It didn't move.

So it wasn't the paper. It was the ink.

Down in the town, St Wenna's clock began to strike four.

On the first stroke, nothing happened. On the second, Mara found she was holding her breath. On the fourth, one of the stitch-marks in the top left corner of the border went pale, like breath fading off a window, and was gone.

She counted. She counted again.

Seventy-one.

"Idris," she said. "Come here."

"Busy."

"*Please.*"

It was the please that did it. He slid out of the lens frame with oil on his cheek and the spanner in his hand, ready to be unimpressed. Then he saw the map.

"Is it a screen?" He reached out a finger.

"Don't!" Mara pulled the map an inch away. "It's paper. It's old. And it's... doing things."

She showed him. The islands. The Lamp Road. The empty gap in the border. She made herself say it in order, the way you'd explain a plan. Idris listened with his head on one side, and for once he didn't say anything clever.

"There's writing," he said instead, pointing.

Along the bottom margin, in faint pencil, someone had written a line in small, spiky letters. Most of it was buried under a brown water stain. Mara bent close.

*If the marks begin to fade, he has* ... and then the stain. Further along: *...Lamp Road...* and at the very end, clear as anything, *E.*

"E.M.," Mara said, and looked at the label on the thread. *Mends what's broken.*

"Who's he?" said Idris. "Who's *he*?"

Before she could answer, the Wren Road changed.

It was the only proper road out of Wrenmouth. It crossed the river on the old stone bridge and climbed up onto the moor, and on the map it was drawn in the shining new ink right to the edge of the paper. Now, as they watched, the part where it crossed the bridge went grey, as if somebody had rubbed it with a thumb. The grey spread. The new line faded. Underneath it, Edie's old black line stayed exactly where it had always been, as if it didn't know anything had happened.

"Um," said a voice from the stairs.

They both jumped.

Bea Nowak stood on the top step with binoculars round her neck, her long black hair blown across her face and a gull feather tucked behind one ear. She was in Mara's class. She hardly ever spoke, and when she did it was usually so quietly that Mr Penrose said, "Pardon?" and Bea didn't say it again.

"Sorry," Bea said. "I was doing the kittiwake count. Only..." She pointed past them, at the landward window. "There's fog on the bridge. Just on the bridge."

Mara and Idris went to the window together.

Below them, Wrenmouth lay in the last of the afternoon light: the roofs, the harbour, the river winding inland. And across the old stone bridge, where cars should have been crawling home for tea, sat a single bank of white fog. It was thick as wool and very still, and nowhere else in the whole valley was there so much as a wisp.

Mara looked from the window to the map and back again.

The grey road and the white fog were exactly the same shape.

## Chapter Two
### Nan's Way Home

Mara went down the lighthouse stairs so fast that she counted them without meaning to. Ninety-nine, a hundred, a hundred and eight. The map was buttoned inside her jacket, flat against her chest, and she could feel the edges of it with every step, as if it had a heartbeat.

Mrs Bello was in the yard with her phone to her ear. "Shut by *what*? It's fog, not a wall."

"I'm going home for tea!" Mara called. Mrs Bello waved without looking. Idris had followed as far as the door, frowning up at the lamp room and turning the spanner over in his hand.

"It said *Lamp Road*," he said. "This is a lamp."

"Don't touch it," said Mara.

"I'm only *thinking* about it."

That, Mara thought, as she ran down the headland path, was exactly what worried her.

Ellery's had a queue out of the door, the way it always did on a Friday. The radio on the shelf behind the counter crackled: "...reports of a sudden fog bank closing the A-road at Wren Bridge. Motorists are advised..."

Dad was at the fryers with his sleeves pushed up and his face shiny with steam. Nan's chair by the till was empty. Her cardigan was on the back of it, and her tea had a skin on it.

"Mara!" Dad's head whipped round. "Is Nan with you?"

Mara's stomach dropped. "No."

"She was right there. I turned my back to do the Coopers' order and..." He tore off his apron. "Jade, mind the fryers. She went up Church Hill last time..."

"I'll do the harbour," said Mara, already moving.

"Ten minutes," Dad shouted after her. "Then I'm phoning..." The door swung shut on the rest.

Mara stopped at the end of the sea front and made herself breathe. Running about shouting was not a plan. Nan didn't wander about at random. Nan went *somewhere*. All this week she had been saying the same thing, brightly, in the voice she used for good news: *I'm meeting Edie at the Palace. Saturday dance.*

There was no Palace in Wrenmouth. There hadn't been for years.

Mara unbuttoned her coat and opened the map against the railings, holding it down against the wind. She ignored the shining green-brown lines and looked at the old black ones underneath. There it was, on the corner where the harbour car park was now: a fat little building with columns drawn on the front and a name in curly letters. *The Palace.*

Nan wasn't wandering. Nan was going somewhere that wasn't there any more, by roads that were.

Mara traced the way with her finger: down Fore Street, through the narrow Ropery Steps, out by the harbour. She ran it. At the bottom she looked across the car park: cars, the pay machine, gulls fighting over a chip paper.

No Nan.

Mara looked at the map again. The Palace's columns faced the water. Its door, drawn as a tiny arch, opened straight onto the stone steps that went down from the harbour wall to the slipway. You came out of the dance, Nan always said, and you could smell the sea.

Mara walked round the end of the last parked car, and there was Nan.

She was sitting on the top step of the harbour wall in her good blue coat and her pink slippers, with her handbag on her knee, watching the lighthouse. Her hair had come loose on one side.

"Nan." Mara sat down beside her, close, so their shoulders touched. Her heart was going so fast it hurt. "Nan, it's me."

Nan turned and looked at her for a long moment. Mara knew that look. It was the look of someone trying to find the right drawer.

Then Nan smiled. "Mara. You've got your grandad's frown on. What's the matter, my love?"

Mara could have cried. She didn't. "You went out without telling Dad."

"Did I? Oh dear. Edie's late anyway." Nan nodded at the lighthouse. "She keeps the light, you know. She's always late on a Friday."

Mara thought about the spiky handwriting on the label. *E.M.* "Nan. Was Edie's other name Marrow?"

"Edie Marrow." Nan said it the way you'd say the name of a song you loved. "Best dancer at the Palace. Couldn't tell you where she came from. She never said."

Mara held out the map. "Is this hers?"

Nan took it on her lap and smoothed it with both hands. Her fingers stopped at the little drawn fish, and she laughed. "That's her hand all right. She drew us that for a joke. *Ellery's, best chips this side of anywhere.*" She frowned at the islands, the shining lines. "Well, she's been busy."

"There's writing," said Mara. "At the bottom. I can't read it."

"Pencil?" Nan tutted. "You don't *look* at pencil, Mara, you tilt it." She lifted the map so that the light from the sky slid sideways across the paper, and the faint grey letters under the brown stain caught the light and shone silver. Mara read them aloud, slowly.

*If the marks begin to fade, he has begun the Stilling. When the last is gone, everything on his Chart will be still. For ever. Go by the Lamp Road to Lantern Quay and ask for the Lamplighters. And remember: he only holds what he has drawn. E.*

The wind blew. A gull complained. Mara read it again in her head and felt cold all the way down.

"Edie used to say a funny thing," said Nan, still looking at the map. "When we were girls. She'd draw a ring round something, a rock pool or a sandcastle, and she'd always leave a little gap. *Never draw a line all the way round, Dot. Leave a gap for the wanderers.*" She smiled. "I never knew what she meant."

Mara looked at the border of the map. Seventy-one stitch-marks. She looked up the valley. The fog still sat on the bridge, white and thick. But now there was another bank further along the coast where the cliff road went, and another, thinner one where the railway curved out of sight behind the hill. On the map, three shining roads had gone grey at the edges of the paper. As she watched, a faint new line of tiny scalloped marks began to draw itself round Wrenmouth, like a fence being put up around a field.

*Everything on his Chart will be still.*

The town was on his Chart. She had watched it being drawn there. The harbour, the shop, Kestrel Rise, the skate park. Nan on her step. Dad running up Church Hill shouting. All of it, still, for ever. Like a photograph you could never step out of.

And for one terrible second, before she could stop it, a small thought crept in. *Nan would never get any worse.*

Mara squashed it flat. She folded the map and stood up and held out her hand.

"Come on, Nan. Dad's looking for you. He's doing his worried face."

"He always did," said Nan, and took her hand.

Dad met them halfway up Fore Street, out of breath, and wrapped his arms round Nan so hard that she said, "Tom, my *hair*." He held Mara's head against his apron and didn't say anything at all for a moment.

Back in the shop, the queue had grown and the phone was ringing. The radio said the coast road was closed now too, and the trains had stopped.

"Dad," Mara said. "Dad, the roads..."

"I know, love. Fog. The Coopers' delivery's stuck up on the moor." He was already back at the fryers. "Can it wait? Please? Just till we're through this lot."

She looked at his face, shiny and tired and frightened from losing Nan. She looked at the map in her hand. She tried to imagine the words that would make him put down the fish slice and listen, and she couldn't find any.

So she took Nan upstairs instead. She made Nan a fresh tea, two sugars, and peeled a new sticky note off her pad. She drew a toothbrush and an arrow and a smiley face, and stuck it on the bathroom door at exactly Nan's eye height.

Then she sat down at the kitchen table and wrote a note on a page from her sketchbook.

> *Dad, I have to fix something. It's important and it's to do with the lighthouse and I'll explain everything. I'll be careful. Nan's blue tablet is at six. I love you. Mara. P.S. Don't be cross.*

She read it twice, then crossed out the P.S., because that was silly. Then she wrote it again underneath, because it wasn't.

She tucked the note under the edge of the till, where Dad always looked when he cashed up. She packed her bag: sketchbook, pencil tin, sticky notes, water bottle, and the big yellow torch from under the sink. The silver thread and the dry pen went in her coat pocket.

"Going back up to help Mrs Bello?" Dad called as she passed the counter. He was wrapping chips without looking. "Here. Take these up for Funmi and the lad. Back by half six."

He pushed a warm paper parcel into her hands. Mara held it against her and nodded, because if she said anything she would say all of it.

It was nearly dark when she reached Gull Head. Mrs Bello's van had gone from the yard. The front door of the lighthouse was open.

And above her, for the first time in Mara's whole life, the light was on.

The beam swept out over the sea, round and round, slow and golden. Mara climbed the hundred and eight steps without counting and came out into the lamp room, into warmth and the smell of paraffin and the steady *tick-tick-tick* of clockwork.

Idris stood beside the turning lens with a can in one hand and the expression of someone who has done something brilliant and is just beginning to wonder if it was also a mistake.

"There was paraffin in the store," he said quickly. "And the clockwork only needed winding. And Mum said not to go anywhere, and I *didn't*..."

"You lit a lighthouse," said Mara.

"It's *really* well made," said Idris.

Bea was there too, at the landward window, with her binoculars. She said something.

"What?" said Mara.

Bea said it again, a little louder. "The kittiwakes. They've gone slow."

Mara didn't understand. Then she looked.

Over Wrenmouth, in the last blue of the evening, the gulls were wheeling over the harbour as they always did, only slowly, like birds in a dream. One beat of their wings took as long as three should have. The smoke from the chimneys on Fore Street was rising in thick, lazy curls that hardly moved at all.

Mara pulled out the map with shaking fingers. Round Wrenmouth, the scalloped fence-line was finished. Inside it, the whole town was drawn in shining ink.

And the Lamp Road was glowing. Not shining like the other lines. *Glowing*, warm and golden, from the lighthouse out across the paper to Lantern Quay, keeping time with the turning of the lamp.

There were two doors in the lamp room. One led to the stairs. The other led out to the iron gallery that ran round the outside of the light, where the keepers used to clean the windows. Mara had put it on her plan that morning: *Gallery door. Rusty. Do not open (Mrs B).*

She walked over to it with the map in her hand.

"Mara," said Idris. "What are you doing?"

"I don't know," Mara said, and that was true too, which frightened her more than anything. She didn't have a plan. She didn't know what was out there. She only knew what the note said, and what the gulls were doing, and who was in the town under them.

She opened the door.

There was no gallery. There was no railing, and no long drop to the rocks. Instead, a road of light ran out from the doorstep, straight and golden and about as wide as a pavement. It led across a sea that wasn't the sea. The water was flat and silver, with mist lying on it like breath on a mirror. At the far end of the road, under a sky the colour of a plum, a harbour glittered with hundreds and hundreds of lamps.

Somewhere, very faintly, bells were ringing.

Idris made a noise that wasn't a word. Then he said, "*I* did that. I lit it. So I'm coming."

"It might not be safe."

"Then you definitely shouldn't go by yourself, should you?"

Behind them, Bea said, "Look."

She said it quietly. But this time both of them heard.

Through the landward window, over the harbour, a single gull hung in the air with its wings spread wide. It wasn't gliding. It wasn't falling. It was simply there, like a paper bird pinned to the sky.

Mara took one step onto the road of light. It was warm through her shoes, and it hummed, like standing on a piano while someone held down a note.

Idris stepped out after her.

Bea looked back at the town for a long moment. Then she came to the doorway and took hold of Mara's sleeve, tight, as if she was afraid of being left behind or of not being let in.

The gallery door swung shut behind them. When Mara turned round, there was nothing there but the lighthouse, standing on its headland at the far end of the road, small and bright and turning.

At the edge of the map, one more stitch-mark faded.

**SEVENTY HOURS REMAINING**

## Chapter Three
### The Lamp Road

The road hummed all the way across.

It was not a loud hum. It was more like the feeling of one, coming up through the soles of Mara's shoes and into her knees. The light wasn't hot, exactly, but it was warm, the way a stone is warm after a sunny day. It was only just wide enough for two people to walk side by side. Nobody suggested walking side by side.

On either side lay the silver sea. Mara kept looking at it and failing to decide what it was. It looked like water, but it didn't splash. It looked like cloud, but it had a surface, smooth as the top of a pond. Very slowly, the whole of it rose and fell, as if something enormous underneath were breathing in its sleep.

"Don't look down," said Idris, looking down.

"I wasn't."

"I was talking to me."

Mara counted steps, because counting things was what she did when the world got too big. Two hundred. Three hundred. At four hundred and twelve, she stopped and unfolded the map.

The Lamp Road glowed across the paper. And on it, a little way out from the lighthouse, were three tiny specks of light, so small they might have been dust. While she watched, they crept forward. Very, very slowly.

She took a step. They moved.

"That's us," she said.

Idris leaned over her shoulder. "We're on the *map*?"

"We're on the road. And the road's on the map." Mara looked along the shining line. There were no other specks on it, but on the islands ahead, there were others: dozens of tiny lights crawling along threads between places, the way ants follow invisible trails. "It shows people. People travelling."

"So someone could watch us," said Bea.

It was the first thing she had said since the lighthouse. All three of them were quiet for a while after that.

The lights of Lantern Quay grew closer. Behind them, the lighthouse grew smaller. When Mara looked back, she could still see Wrenmouth beyond it: a dark huddle of roofs with a few lit windows. A gull hung over the headland, its wings spread, not moving at all.

"It's like the town is holding its breath," Bea said.

"It's not *stopped*," Idris said. "Look. The lighthouse beam's still going round."

Mara watched it. For a long time it didn't seem to move at all. Then she was almost sure that it had crept a finger's width across the sky since they'd set out.

She found that she was holding the chip parcel very tightly against her chest. It was still warm. She thought about Dad wrapping it without looking, and the note under the till, and Nan's sticky note on the bathroom door. She thought about Dad finding the note and wondered how long it would take him in that slowed-down town. Then she decided not to think about it at all.

"Who's hungry?" she said.

They ate Dad's chips walking along a road of light over a breathing sea, passing the parcel from hand to hand. They were too hot, and then just right, and properly salty, with plenty of vinegar soaked into the paper at the bottom.

"These," said Idris, with his mouth full, "are the best chips I have ever had in my life."

"It's because we're scared," said Bea.

"No. It's because my dad's brilliant," said Mara, and the others didn't argue.

Afterwards, Idris folded the empty paper into a neat square and put it in his pocket. "Don't want to drop litter," he said. "In another world." Then: "We should've told someone. A grown-up. My mum could..."

"Your mum's stuck behind a fog bank."

"Your dad, then."

"I *tried*," said Mara, and it came out sharper than she meant it to. Idris shut his mouth. They walked on in a silence that was a different shape from before.

Lantern Quay was a harbour made of lamps.

There were lamps on poles along the stone quayside, lamps strung in loops between the houses, lamps in every window and on the prow of every boat. There were brass lamps and paper lamps, lamps of green glass and blue glass, and one enormous lamp in a tower at the end of the harbour wall whose light was the same gold as the road. People were climbing tall ladders to trim wicks, and a few walked on long wooden stilts, which looked faster. Everyone, Mara noticed, was carrying a light of some kind, even in the brightness. It smelled of lamp oil and frying fish skins and seaweed.

The road of light ended at the bottom of a flight of stone steps. Standing on the top step, holding a long pole with a hook at the end and a small tin lantern hanging from it, was a boy.

He was about Idris's age and about half his width, with copper curls stuffed under a floppy blue cap, a brown scarf wound up to his freckled nose, and a smudge of soot on one cheek. He was staring at them as if they were a ghost who had turned up for tea.

"The Lamp Road's been dead forty years," he said. "Who are you? Who sent you? And why does it smell of..." He sniffed. "What *is* that?"

"Chips," said Idris.

"What's a chips?"

"Fried potato. In paper."

The boy considered this. "In paper," he said. "That's genius." Then he remembered he was supposed to be suspicious and pointed the hook-pole at them. "Right. State your business. I'm in charge of this end."

"Are you?" said Bea.

He opened his mouth, then closed it. "Basically," he said.

Mara didn't have time for *basically*. She held up the map.

"It says to ask for the Lamplighters," she said. "There's a man called the Surveyor, and he's started something called the Stilling, and my whole town is on his Chart, and it's slowing down, and in seventy hours it's going to stop for ever. This is Edie Marrow's map. So please, if you're not in charge, take us to whoever is."

The boy's face changed. All the cleverness went out of it.

"Edie Marrow's," he said. "Her *actual* one."

He looked over his shoulder at the great lamp tower. Then he looked back at Mara, and for a second he seemed much younger than eleven.

"I'm Tavi," he said. "Come on. And don't let anyone else see that."

Ro Sallow, Head Lamplighter of Lantern Quay, was halfway up the inside of the great lamp tower when they found her, trimming a wick as long as Mara's arm. She was tall and grey-haired, with forearms like ship's ropes and a face that looked as if it had not slept for a week. She came down the ladder when Tavi called, wiped her hands, looked at the map for a long time, and then looked at Mara for even longer.

"Edie's girl?" she said.

"No. My nan was her friend. Edie died. When I was four."

Ro closed her eyes briefly. "I'd wondered. When the road went dark." She sat down heavily on an upturned crate. "Sit. All of you. Tavi, put the kettle on."

"But..."

"*Kettle*, Tavi."

He went.

What Ro told them, Mara wrote down afterwards in her sketchbook, so that she wouldn't lose any of it.

The Stilling had begun at three o'clock that afternoon, exactly. Everybody on the Quay had felt it, like a door shutting somewhere far away. The Surveyor had posted notices all over the Fold that morning. Ro pulled one off the wall and handed it to Mara. It was printed in small, neat letters:

> NOTICE
> By order of the Surveyor, and for the safety of all.
> Certain ways will close. A schedule is attached.
> BE AT HOME.
> At three o'clock on Monday, all will be still, and all will be safe.

"Still," said Idris. "What does still actually *mean*?"

"It means stopped," said Ro. "Every island on his Chart, every soul on them. Like flies in amber. Not dead. Just... fixed. In one moment, inside his map. For ever."

"But why's *my* town on it?" Mara said. "We're not even in your... your..."

"The Fold." Ro tapped the glowing Lamp Road with one thick finger. "Because of this. Edie built this road between her lamp and mine, years ago. It was hers. He never had it on his Chart. But this summer, someone found it, and he drew it in. And a road on his Chart has to *end* somewhere on his Chart. So he drew the other end, too." She looked at Mara. "Your town."

Mara thought about the men with the brass chains, clinking like money along the harbour wall.

"It's slowing," Bea said. "Our town. The birds have stopped."

"It would." Ro's voice gentled. "New places sink into his Chart slow, like a stone into honey. Our islands have been on it for hundreds of years, so they'll stop all at once, at the end. Yours is sinking already." She did a sum on her fingers. "By the time it's finished, your people will have lived about an hour. They won't feel a thing until the last."

"So we have to stop him," said Mara.

Ro was quiet. Tavi came back with four mugs of something that smelled like smoky tea and handed them round without making a single joke.

"What you have to do," Ro said at last, "is go home. Tonight. While my lamp's lit and yours is too, the road's open. Go back to your families."

"My family's on his Chart," said Mara.

"So's mine. So's everyone's." Ro rubbed her face. "Do you think I wouldn't go? If my lamp goes dark, your road home goes dark with it. There's two old lamplighters on this Quay who can still climb that ladder, and I'm one of them. I can't leave it. Not for an hour."

"Then tell us how to stop him," said Mara. Her voice wobbled. She didn't let it.

Ro opened her mouth to answer.

Across the water, the bells began.

Not a tune. Seven slow notes, each one left to fade completely before the next. Everyone on the Quay outside stopped talking. Through the tower's open door, Mara saw people standing still in the lamplight with their faces turned east.

"Lost-road toll," said Tavi quietly. "Bellbridge rings it when a way's gone."

Mara spread the map across her knees. Far to the east, past islands she didn't know the names of yet, a line between a tall, thin tower and a domed building half-drawn in water was turning grey. As she watched, the grey thinned to nothing. Where the line had been, there was only empty paper.

The seventh bell faded.

"He's started closing the roads," said Ro.

Mara looked up from the map. "Then we can't wait till morning to start."

"You can't cross Bellbridge till first bell. They lock the gates at dark, Stilling or no Stilling." Ro stood up. She went to a shelf at the back of the tower, took down a tin box and opened it. Inside was a single envelope, yellow with age, addressed in a spiky hand Mara already knew.

"I can't tell you how to stop him," Ro said. "But Edie might." She held out her other hand. "Tavi. Fetch your lantern."

## Chapter Four
### What the Lantern Heard

"It has to be dark," said Tavi. "Properly dark. Not *shady*. Dark."

Ro took them into the oil store at the bottom of the tower, a round stone room that smelled like a garage, and shut the door. Tavi closed the shutter on the lantern's own small flame until only a thread of light showed, and held it close to Edie's letter where it lay on an upturned barrel.

"How does it work?" Idris whispered.

"Things remember sounds," Tavi said. "Not much. Just the loudest bit, the bit that mattered. The lantern lets them say it. Once." He tapped the fat candle inside. It was marked with six notches, like a ruler. "Each echo burns one mark. When it's gone, it's gone. And you can't ask the same thing twice. So nobody sneeze."

Mara held her breath. The thread of light flickered.

Then a woman's voice filled the oil store. It was dry, quick and amused, close enough to make Mara jump.

*"...and if he ever begins it, Ro, the answer's where he and I learned our letters. Under the water, behind the stars. And Ro, don't let anyone draw the last line. Not him. Not anyone."*

The voice stopped. The candle had burned down past its first notch, and the wick smoked.

Ro stood very still in the dark. Then she opened the door, and lamplight came back in, and she was just a tired woman wiping her eyes with the back of her wrist.

"Well," she said. "That's Edie. Never once said a thing straight."

---

Supper was fish stew with lumps of something Tavi called fogcake, which was pale and spongy and tasted, Idris said, like a cloud that had been fried. They ate at Ro's kitchen table under a ceiling hung with lamps. Bea opened a battered tin she had been carrying all along in her coat pocket and shared out a flapjack. It turned out to be the best thing anyone had eaten since the chips.

Mara laid the map out between the bowls and asked her questions in order. She had written them in her sketchbook while the stew was heating.

Why had the map been blank, apart from Wrenmouth?

"Edie cut it loose," said Ro. "A copy's tied to his Chart, both ways. What's on his shows on yours. But a copy left blank long enough can't be read from the other end. She let it go empty so he could never look through it. Then he drew her road onto his Chart, and her map was joined to his again, through the road. So it filled up."

What was the pen?

Ro turned it over, scraped the dried crust on the nib with her thumbnail and sniffed. "Tide-ink. Long dry." She looked at Mara hard. "Listen. That's the one thing you must never do. Tide-ink on a copy doesn't stay on the copy. It goes to *him*. Whatever you draw, he gets."

Mara nodded and wrote it down, and underlined it twice.

Why had he never started the Stilling before?

Ro was quiet a long time. "I don't know," she said at last. "Forty years he's talked about safety. Forty years nothing. Then this summer his Chainmen find Edie's road, and three months later he begins." She shook her head. "Edie knew something. Edie always knew something."

She pinned the Surveyor's schedule flat on the table, and Mara copied it out:

> *Observatory Causeway: closed. Friday, seven o'clock.*
> *Glasswater Causeway: Saturday, one o'clock.*
> *The Ropewalk: Saturday, four o'clock.*
> *Bellbridge: Sunday, twelve noon.*
> *Marsh Pole Line: Sunday, six o'clock.*
> *The Chainway: Monday, two o'clock.*
> *The Lamp Road and the Rim Road will remain open until the Stilling.*
> *Each way will fade during the hour before it closes. Do not be upon it.*

"He tells everyone when," said Bea, surprised.

"He doesn't want anyone caught between places," said Ro. "He thinks that's kindness."

Then Ro showed them where everything was. Lantern Quay on its rocky island. Bellbridge, running east to the big middle island. The Whispering Canopy to the north of it, the Glasswater Marsh to the south. The Upside-Down Orchard on its floating crag in the north-east, and, far away on the eastern edge, the Surveyor's Tower, tall and thin.

"Under the water," said Idris, tapping a domed shape half-sunk in a lagoon in the south-east. "Flooded Observatory. Got to be."

Mara bent closer. Below the Observatory, the bottom right-hand corner of the map was missing. Not stained like the Canopy: torn clean away, leaving a ragged edge. Part of the legend had gone with it.

"Maybe," she said.

"*Maybe?* It's flooded! It's literally under the water!"

"Lots of things are under water. The marsh is under water." Mara's finger moved. "And what does *behind the stars* mean? If we go all that way and it's wrong, we've wasted a whole day." She turned to Ro. "Is there anyone who knew Edie and the Surveyor? When they were learning?"

"Amma Oduya," said Tavi at once. "At the Orchard. She's the oldest person in the Fold. She knows everyone's business back to the beginning of time."

"Then we ask her first," said Mara. "Then we know. Then we go."

Idris stared at her. "Bellbridge, Glasswater Causeway, Pole Line." He traced it with a finger. "We'd be at the Observatory by *lunchtime*."

"And if it's the wrong place, we'd be stuck at the bottom of the map with the roads closing behind us." Mara heard her own voice go hard and tight, the way it did when she was frightened of being wrong. "We need to be sure."

"Tavi?" said Ro.

Tavi shrugged too quickly. "Orchard's a good idea. Amma Oduya sells to the Night Market. She hears things." He didn't look at anyone when he said it.

Bea looked at Idris's finger on the map, and opened her mouth, and closed it again.

"Two against one," said Mara.

"Bea didn't vote," said Idris.

Bea looked down at her stew.

Ro sighed. "Tavi goes with you. He knows the customs and he can work a lantern. Tavi, no showing off."

"Me?" said Tavi, in the voice of someone who had been hoping for exactly this since the moment they arrived. "Oh, *fine*."

---

Idris was quiet for a while after that. When Ro went to make cocoa, he picked up her teapot, a fat brown thing with a crack running down the spout, the kind that dribbled. He looked at it. He looked at the spool of silver thread that Mara had left on the table.

"Idris..."

He had already wound a length round the spout, neat and tight, five or six turns. The thread glowed faintly. When he unwound it, there was no thread left and no crack either: just smooth brown glaze, as if the teapot had never been dropped in its life.

"*Mends what's broken*," he said, grinning. "Ro's been good to us."

Mara grabbed the spool and measured it against her ruler, end to end, her heart thumping. She had measured it in the lighthouse. Two hundred centimetres. Now it was a hundred and eighty.

"That's not ours to waste," she said.

"It's a teapot. It's *fixed*."

"It's a *teapot*. We might need that for a bridge, or a boat, or..." She didn't know what. That was the point. She didn't know, and he hadn't stopped to wonder. "You never think."

"And you never stop thinking," said Idris, and went out to the step.

Ro came back with the cocoa, looked at the teapot, then at the two of them, and said nothing at all. But she poured Idris's cocoa first, from the mended spout, and it didn't dribble.

---

They slept in the tower in a row of hammocks. Bea had a sticky note from Mara's pad and was writing on it by lamplight in tiny letters: *Mum, Jo, Kit. I'm all right. I'm sorry.* She folded it very small and put it in her flapjack tin.

"You can't send it," Mara whispered.

"I know," said Bea. "I just wanted it to exist."

Mara couldn't sleep. Near midnight she climbed the tower stair to the lamp gallery with the map, and Ro was there, trimming the great wick.

From up here, the whole harbour glittered. A fishing boat was pulling away from the quay, its stern lamp swinging. Mara watched it on the map: a speck sliding along the dotted ferry line out of the harbour mouth. Then the boat turned off the line, out into the open silver. Through the window, she could still see its lamp bobbing on the Slack, clear as anything.

On the map, its speck had gone.

She looked from window to paper, paper to window. She drew a small pencil note in her sketchbook: *Off the road = not on the map?*

"Can't sleep?" said Ro.

"Everywhere's so bright here," said Mara.

Ro smiled and pointed straight down, over the rail, to the foot of the tower. All around it the quay blazed with light. But in a ring right under the great lamp, where the tower's own shadow fell, there was a pool of black.

"Lamplighters' saying," Ro said. "It's always darkest under the lamp. Go to bed, Mara."

At a quarter to six, in the grey before first bell, they walked out along the harbour to the gates of Bellbridge: Mara with the map buttoned in her jacket, Idris with his tool roll strapped across his chest and Mrs Bello's blue rope over his shoulder, Bea with her binoculars, and Tavi with his lantern swinging from its pole.

The bridge gate was still locked. Beside it, a man in a long grey coat stood waiting. Brass links hung from his belt and clinked when he turned his head to watch them come.

"Chainman," breathed Tavi.

The man smiled politely and looked straight at Mara's jacket, as if he could see through the buttons.
